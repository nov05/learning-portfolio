I'm Wenjing Liu. I worked as an SAP implementation technical consultant from 2005 to 2016, and I now work in machine learning and data science. This gives me a practical view of enterprise systems and a newer perspective on technology.  

I joined this program to develop the business vocabulary to communicate technical ideas to a broader audience, and better understand how technology, business strategy, and customer interests intersect in enterprise software.  

For example, why might a company's goals conflict with the interests of its customers and ecosystem? Why might production standardization come at the cost of customer autonomy? And why might the business narrative differ from operational reality?

I evaluated SAP because its core product provides the transactional backbone for the global economy. Its “RISE with SAP” cloud initiative represents a fundamental conflict over system control. 

SAP is mandating customers to adopt a standardized “Clean Core” to simplify its cloud upgrade paths, while enterprise legacy systems depend on complex custom development. 

Moving to RISE strips clients of direct OS- and database-level access, replacing local Basis troubleshooting with managed-service SLA ticket queues. 

As customization moves outside the SAP ERP system core, customers become more dependent on released APIs. When APIs don't cover a required capability, or aren't available quickly enough, existing custom development may need to be redesigned as BTP extensions, adding integration and engineering costs. 

When a massive software monopoly reduces clients' control of their own systems, the impact extends beyond IT and can erode trust across the entire ERP ecosystem.

The impact on customers is visible in four areas.  
  
Customization Agility declines as a standardized SAP ERP system core makes it harder to adapt systems locally.  
  
Migration Burden is substantial, with high costs, long timelines, and operational risks for complex legacy systems.  
  
Cost Expansion comes from “Clean Core”, cloud transformation, and BTP-based extensions, creating additional implementation, integration, and modernization costs for customers.  
  
Finally, Deprived Basis Control means losing direct OS and database access and relying on managed-service processes for routine operations.  

The synthesis is clear: SAP has strengthened its financial model, while the transformation shifts more cost, complexity, and operational control onto customers.

SAP's “Clean Core” strategy pressures customers to reduce legacy customization, while they bear the cost of deciding what to preserve, redesign, or move outside the core. The standoffs between developer groups and SAP show that cloud migration is not simply a hosting change. It can be a high-risk redesign of complex legacy systems.  
  
To protect customers, the focus should be on maintaining a buffer and preserving autonomy.  
  
First, customers should not be forced into a Greenfield approach. RISE may offer a Brownfield path, but for deeply customized systems, it needs to remain genuinely viable in practice. On-Premise with Third-Party Support can provide an ultimate exit option. Together, these options give customers room to choose their own pace and path.  
  
Second, customers need Operational Autonomy. Their own Basis teams should retain self-service control over routine technical operations.  
  
Finally, customers need an open integration path beyond proprietary BTP. As more system customization moves outside the SAP ERP system core, APIs become critical for connecting custom business logic, ML applications, AI agents, and external systems back to the ERP. Keeping OData and REST APIs available gives customers an escape hatch, rather than making BTP the only practical gateway.  

Technology is an ecosystem too. It grows organically out of complex realities, and it cannot always be redesigned from the top down.
