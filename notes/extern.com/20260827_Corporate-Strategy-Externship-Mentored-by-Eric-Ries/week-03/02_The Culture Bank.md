# The Culture Bank

**How Trust Is Built and Spent in Organizations**

*Estimated reading time: 15 minutes*

## Why This, Why Now?

You're about to propose an idea for strengthening a company. Good ideas fail all the time—not because they're wrong, but because they don't build trust. Eric Ries's Culture Bank concept explains how organizations build and spend trust over time. Understanding this will help you propose ideas that create lasting deposits, not just short-term fixes.

## What Is the Culture Bank?

In *Incorruptible*, Eric Ries uses the metaphor of a "culture bank" to describe how trust and commitment accumulate in organizations.

**The core idea:** Every action either deposits into or withdraws from the culture bank.

| Deposits                                             | Withdrawals                        |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Following through on commitments                     | Breaking promises, even small ones |
| Admitting mistakes transparently                     | Hiding problems or shifting blame  |
| Making decisions aligned with stated values          | Making exceptions “just this once” |
| Investing in long-term mission over short-term gains | Sacrificing mission for quick wins |
| Including stakeholders in decisions                  | Making decisions without input     |

> **The Insight**
>
> Organizations can spend their culture bank balance when times get tough—but only if they've made enough deposits. Companies that overdraw their culture bank lose the trust needed to navigate challenges.

## From the Book: *Incorruptible*, Chapter 8: “The Invisible Leader”

> **The Culture Bank**
>
> That H-E-B store manager made a big deposit in the company's culture bank, the metaphorical accounting system I introduced above that tracks actions that either build or deplete organizational trustworthiness. This is a way of thinking about culture that I first learned from pioneers of strong culture companies like Devoted Health's Todd Park and Starbucks' Howard Schultz. The idea is simple but powerful: every action either builds or degrades culture. The actions that strengthen culture, leading to a more trustworthy organization, are "deposits." Opposite actions are "withdrawals."
>
> Many culture bank deposits have easily identifiable costs in terms of R&D, investing in employees, or making things right for an unhappy customer. When H-E-B absorbs losses on eggs to protect low-income families, or gives away groceries in a storm, these costs show up immediately on the balance sheet. But the payback, the invisible trust inspired by the costly choice, compounds like interest. Over time it manifests as customer loyalty that survives price increases, employee retention through tough times, and community support when you need permits for a new store.
>
> By framing trust in banking terms—deposits, withdrawals, balances—we create terminology that can compete with financial metrics in decision-making. When faced with a trade-off between short-term gains and long-term trust, employees now have a familiar framework: Would this be a deposit or a withdrawal?
>
> The culture bank exposes the great flaw in the modern system of objectives and key results (OKRs). When given a quantitative objective, employees can willfully and repeatedly boost their personal numbers by borrowing against the company's trustworthiness. Even worse, in a competitive compensation environment, those who refuse to make these trades are at a permanent disadvantage. The OKR system rewards those who spend down the company's trust without even tracking that it's happening. The culture bank provides a counterweight. Ship buggy software to hit the deadline? That's a withdrawal. Oversell a product's capabilities to close the quarter? Another withdrawal.
>
> The next time someone proposes damaging your company's long-term health by acting in an untrustworthy manner, you'll see it more clearly. This is not about hardheaded "making money" versus softhearted "caring." This person is proposing the liquidation of a critical asset. Say so.

## Deposits and Withdrawals at Scale

The Culture Bank operates at multiple levels:

| Level          | Example Deposits                        | Example Withdrawals                      |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **Individual** | A manager admits they were wrong        | A leader takes credit for others' work   |
| **Team**       | A team celebrates learning from failure | A team blames a scapegoat for problems   |
| **Company**    | CEO takes a pay cut during layoffs      | Leadership gets bonuses during layoffs   |
| **Ecosystem**  | Company shares knowledge openly         | Company hoards information for advantage |

## The Compound Interest of Deposits

Small deposits, made consistently, create compound returns:

| Week 1 Deposit                 | Week 4 Effect                     | Month 6 Effect                  |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Manager admits a mistake       | Team feels safer raising concerns | Culture of psychological safety |
| Company shares a failure story | Others start sharing too          | Learning culture takes root     |
| Leader asks for feedback       | Others realize input is welcome   | Inclusive decision-making norm  |

**Design for compounding:**

The best proposals don't just make one deposit—they create systems that generate ongoing deposits. A one-time initiative is less powerful than a repeating ritual.

## TL;DR

* The Culture Bank is a metaphor for how trust accumulates or depletes in organizations.
* Every action is either a deposit, building trust, or a withdrawal, spending trust.
* Good proposals create clear deposits—they build trust, not just solve problems.
* The best proposals create systems that generate ongoing deposits, not just one-time fixes.
* Think about how your proposal makes deposits in your chosen lens.
