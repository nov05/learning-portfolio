# Transmission Multipliers

**How Ideas Spread Through Organizations**

*Estimated reading time: 15 minutes*

## Why This, Why Now?

Good proposals don't succeed simply because they're good ideas. They succeed because they spread. In *Incorruptible* (p. 307), Eric Ries explains how certain people, practices, and moments amplify cultural signals throughout an organization. Understanding Transmission Multipliers will help you design proposals that stick and spread—not just get heard once and forgotten.

## What Are Transmission Multipliers?

**The core idea:** Not all influence is equal. Some people, practices, and moments have an outsized influence on how ideas spread through an organization.

| Type          | Example                     | Why It Multiplies                                   |
| ------------- | --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **People**    | A respected senior engineer | When they endorse an idea, others take it seriously |
| **Practices** | Weekly all-hands meeting    | Repeated rituals spread messages to everyone        |
| **Moments**   | How a crisis is handled     | High-visibility events send powerful signals        |

> **The Insight**
>
> One transmission multiplier can spread an idea further than a hundred emails. Your proposal should leverage these amplifiers.

## From the Book: *Incorruptible*, Chapter 12: “Mission Transmission”

> **Transmission Multipliers**
>
> To understand how this transmission works, we need to understand two aspects of it. First, transmission happens automatically, using the same rules as gravity that we first encountered in Chapter 3, every time an organization conducts a transaction. Thus, transmission happens via the same "fiduciary" pathways we encountered in Chapter 7: via customers, employees, partners, and communities.
>
> Second, it's important to understand the mechanisms that companies can intentionally wield to magnify these effects. I call these practices *transmission multipliers*. Let me illustrate. I once worked with a company that had to scan local obituaries to find new customers. No, they weren't selling funeral services. Their clients were municipal agencies so locked into long-standing vendors that they wouldn't consider alternatives until someone retired or died.
>
> The private sector is just as guilty. The father of modern management science, W. Edwards Deming, urged in 1982, "End the practice of awarding business on the basis of price tag." Yet most organizations still default to the cheapest, most established, or most popular vendor, actively choosing convenience over mission. Again, because every purchase, partnership, and contract transmits values, these transactions often exacerbate the problems organizations exist to solve.
>
> The following five practices can ensure any organization transmits, rather than undercuts, its mission through every transaction:
>
> **1. Consider mission in every transaction.** Whenever money, power, or status changes hands, ask whether mission and ethos were considered alongside conventional factors. This sounds obvious, but when I talk with leaders about whether they did it before deciding to contract with a vendor or enter into a partnership, you'd be amazed at how often I hear: "It didn't come up." Build the practice into systems by adding mission criteria to requests for proposals (RFPs), vendor scorecards, and partnership agreements. Evaluate mission alignment as rigorously as other factors.
>
> **2. Make mission the tiebreaker.** When all other factors are roughly equal, values alignment should determine the choice. Quality and price still matter; mission serves only as the decisive factor in close calls. Sometimes a mission-aligned vendor genuinely can't meet critical requirements or their costs are prohibitive. When this happens, document this as an ecosystem failing and look for opportunities to invest in building better alternatives… When Patagonia invested millions in helping suppliers convert to organic cotton in the 1990s with no guarantee of success, they built a network that now leads the industry.
>
> **3. Clear the path for allies.** Procurement departments are overwhelmed partly because they've created byzantine systems that tend to lock in the incumbent. Large companies have entire teams dedicated to navigating these complex mazes, including proposal writers, compliance officers, and contract specialists. Meanwhile, mission-aligned startups have founders trying to do everything. The best organizations recognize this imbalance and actively correct it. Toyota sends its own engineers to teach smaller suppliers the Toyota Production System at Toyota's expense.
>
> **4. Signal with costly commitment.** Remember that gravity is shaped by perception. People have to believe in the organization's commitment, and the most credible way to accomplish this is through actions that require real resources. At dinner several years ago, Thomas Friedman and I were debating well into the night the best way for Airbnb to achieve this kind of transmission. The Pulitzer Prize–winning columnist made a memorable point about trust that I'll never forget: "I need to see blood on the floor!"
>
> **5. Create mechanisms for recourse.** Give external partners real channels for redress when promises aren't met. Costco provides a confidential ethics hotline that routes directly to their general counsel and chief compliance officer, creating an escalation path outside of day-to-day buying teams. A partnership called the Fair Food Program persuaded major food buyers including Walmart and McDonald's to voluntarily sign binding agreements to follow a code of conduct that protects farmworkers from harsh working conditions and sexual assault, resulting in over 2,100 complaints investigated and resolved with no retaliation… Patagonia goes even further. For every Fairtrade-certified item sold, Patagonia pays an additional premium on top of the regular price, which goes directly to the workers themselves… Since 2014, more than $40 million has been invested in health care, education, cash bonuses, and community projects. In one Sri Lankan facility, workers chose to build and staff an on-site childcare center—an echo of Patagonia's own childcare philosophy (pioneered by Malinda Chouinard in 1985). This is mission transmission in action.
>
> These mechanisms create trust not just through their existence, but through their visible use—or nonuse. The organizations that master this practice understand that giving away power paradoxically increases their influence.

## People as Transmission Multipliers

Certain people have an outsized influence on what spreads.

### The Respected Veteran

**Who they are:** Someone with tenure who is respected by peers—not just management.

**Why they matter:** When they model behavior or endorse ideas, others follow. Their approval signals, “This is how we do things here.”

**How to leverage them:** Suggest involving a respected veteran in launching or championing the initiative.

### The Connector

**Who they are:** Someone who knows everyone and facilitates introductions.

**Why they matter:** They shape who talks to whom. Information flows through them.

**How to leverage them:** Design proposals that connectors can easily share and discuss.

### The Early Adopter

**Who they are:** Someone who tries new things before others.

**Why they matter:** They de-risk new ideas. Once they try something, others feel safer following.

**How to leverage them:** Include an early-adopter pilot phase where willing participants can test the idea first.

### Leadership — Formal Authority

**Who they are:** People with titles—managers, directors, and executives.

**Why they matter:** Their endorsement grants legitimacy. Their resistance can kill ideas.

**How to leverage them:** Think about which leaders would need to support the idea and how to frame it for them.

## Practices as Transmission Multipliers

Repeating rituals embed ideas into organizational culture.

| Practice                 | How It Multiplies                        | Example Application                                                   |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Onboarding**           | Shapes new employee beliefs from day one | Add mission discussion to new-hire orientation                        |
| **All-hands meetings**   | Reaches everyone at once                 | Add a recurring agenda item that reinforces your idea                 |
| **Performance reviews**  | Signals what's valued                    | Add a criterion related to your lens                                  |
| **Team retrospectives**  | Creates space for reflection             | Add a discussion question that surfaces your theme                    |
| **Recognition programs** | Shows what gets rewarded                 | Tie recognition to your lens, such as “Mission Champion of the Month” |

> **Design for Repetition**
>
> A one-time announcement fades. A repeating ritual reinforces. The best proposals attach to existing practices or create new rituals.

## Moments as Transmission Multipliers

Some moments have an outsized influence on organizational culture.

| Moment                      | Why It Matters                                    | Proposal Opportunity                                                 |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Crises and setbacks**     | How problems are handled reveals true priorities  | Propose a post-incident reflection process that reinforces values    |
| **Major decisions**         | Big choices signal what's really valued           | Propose a decision-making framework that explicitly weighs mission   |
| **Departures**              | How people leave tells everyone about the culture | Propose exit interview practices that capture mission feedback       |
| **Celebrations and wins**   | What gets celebrated defines what matters         | Propose celebration rituals tied to mission impact, not just metrics |
| **Onboarding / first days** | First impressions shape long-term perception      | Propose onboarding touchpoints that emphasize incorruptibility       |

## How This Applies to Your Proposal

Your improvement proposal should leverage transmission multipliers. Ask yourself:

### Multiplier Mapping for Your Idea

| Multiplier Type | Questions to Ask                                                          | Your Thoughts |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- |
| **People**      | Who would be credible champions for this idea? Who needs to endorse it?   |               |
| **Practices**   | What existing rituals could carry this idea? What new rituals would help? |               |
| **Moments**     | What upcoming events or decisions could reinforce this idea?              |               |

### Example Applications by Lens

| Lens                     | Multiplier-Leveraging Proposal Example                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Mission Drive**        | Create a “Mission Minute” segment at weekly all-hands where someone shares a story of mission in action *(practice multiplier)*        |
| **Financial Gravity**    | Have the CFO publicly explain quarterly financial decisions through a mission lens at town halls *(people + practice multiplier)*      |
| **Governance**           | Introduce a “stakeholder advocate” role that participates in key decisions *(people + moment multiplier)*                              |
| **Mission Transmission** | Add a “values reflection” exercise to the onboarding program led by respected, long-tenured employees *(people + practice multiplier)* |

## Designing for Spread

When crafting your proposal, check whether it has these transmission-friendly qualities:

| Quality                      | Why It Helps                       | Example                                                         |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Simple to explain**        | Easy to pass on in conversation    | “We're adding a 2-minute mission check to our Monday meetings.” |
| **Visible**                  | Others can see it happening        | A public Slack channel, wall display, or recurring meeting      |
| **Repeatable**               | Creates ongoing touchpoints        | Weekly, monthly, or quarterly rituals                           |
| **Social**                   | Involves multiple people           | Group discussions, team reflections, peer recognition           |
| **Tied to existing rhythms** | Doesn't require new infrastructure | Add to existing meetings or use existing tools                  |

> **The Multiplier Test**
>
> Ask yourself: **“How would someone who wasn't in the room find out about this?”**
>
> If there's no clear answer, your idea might not spread.

## TL;DR

* Transmission Multipliers are people, practices, and moments that amplify ideas through organizations.
* People multipliers include respected veterans, connectors, early adopters, and leaders.
* Practice multipliers include onboarding, meetings, reviews, and recognition programs.
* Moment multipliers include crises, major decisions, departures, and celebrations.
* Design proposals that leverage multipliers—make them simple, visible, repeatable, and social.
* Ask: **“How would someone who wasn't in the room find out about this?”**
