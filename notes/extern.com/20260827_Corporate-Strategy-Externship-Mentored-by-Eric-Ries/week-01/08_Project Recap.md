👉 My answers:

1. If your project had a highlight reel, what would be on it?

I would highlight the moment when a company I initially picked almost at random turned into a much bigger story. I started with Anthology, but as I collected more information, I realized that Anthology was essentially a “Frankenstein” assembled through acquisitions, and that the more interesting story was Blackboard itself. I discovered that both Blackboard and several of the businesses involved in the later consolidation had gone through private-equity ownership before being brought together. The timing also made me think more seriously about how difficult business forecasting can be: major acquisitions around the COVID period were made in an environment where many people expected remote learning and remote work to become permanent, while much of the market ultimately returned to schools and offices. That raised questions about how acquisition teams estimated markets, business prospects, and valuations. Once I found the combination of high leverage and declining EBITDA, I started looking beyond the company itself and thinking about how interest rates and the broader financial environment shaped the story. The highlight for me was finally putting all of these pieces together into a coherent timeline and uncovering the Blackboard story behind Anthology.

2. Which skill do you feel you leveled up the most during this project? Tell us how you noticed the growth. It can be anything — from soft skills like communication to technical skills like using a new tool.

The biggest skill I developed was financial and business research, especially the ability to reconstruct a company's history from fragmented information. I became much better at connecting ownership changes, acquisitions, debt, EBITDA, interest rates, and broader market conditions instead of looking at each fact in isolation. I noticed the growth when I was able to move from simply collecting facts about Anthology to building a timeline and identifying the underlying financial and governance pattern.

3. What was the hardest part — and how did you push through it?

The hardest part was dealing with incomplete information, particularly because private-equity ownership can make important financial and ownership details much less transparent. The second challenge was that I am not a professional financial analyst, so there was a limit to how much time I could realistically spend reconstructing and analyzing every financial detail. I pushed through by focusing on the key relationships rather than trying to model everything perfectly: ownership changes, leverage, EBITDA, interest rates, acquisitions, and the sequence of events. Eventually, I was able to identify the main direction of the story and build a timeline that made the different pieces fit together.

4. If you could tweak one part of the project to make it even better, what would you change — and why?

I would give participants more time and better guidance for analyzing the financial and macroeconomic dimensions of their case. The four-lens framework is useful for identifying the structural problem, but understanding Financial Gravity in a real company can require reconstructing years of debt, EBITDA, interest rates, ownership changes, and acquisition activity. More guidance on which financial data to prioritize—and how to distinguish company-specific decisions from broader market forces—would make the analysis more rigorous without requiring participants to become professional financial analysts.
