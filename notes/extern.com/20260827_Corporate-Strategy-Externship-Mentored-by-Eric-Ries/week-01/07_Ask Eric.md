1. **How should the Incorruptible framework account for macroeconomic and geopolitical forces?**

    When diagnosing a company's failure, how much weight should we give to factors outside the company's control, such as interest rates, credit cycles, regulation, geopolitical shocks, or broader changes in capital markets?

    I encountered this question while analyzing Blackboard / Anthology. The company's trajectory cannot be understood entirely through company-level decisions: the broader credit environment also affected the economics of private-equity ownership. In particular, periods of low interest rates and abundant credit can make highly leveraged acquisitions more attractive by lowering the cost of debt and allowing sponsors to finance a larger portion of an acquisition with borrowed capital. This made me wonder how the framework should incorporate macroeconomic conditions when those conditions directly shape the financial incentives and capital structures available to companies and their owners.

    Across regions, data suggest that U.S. buyouts have used the most excess leverage, followed by Europe, while buyouts in the rest of the world have used much less relative to comparable public companies. MSCI, “Leverage in Private Equity: What Do We Know?” https://www.msci.com/research-and-insights/blog-post/leverage-in-private-equity-what-do-we-know

    This is consistent with Axelson, Jenkinson, Strömberg, and Weisbach’s study of 1,157 worldwide buyouts from 1980–2008, which found that economy-wide borrowing costs were a major driver of both the amount and composition of buyout debt, while credit conditions also affected acquisition prices. NBER, “Borrow Cheap, Buy High? The Determinants of Leverage and Pricing in Buyouts” https://www.nber.org/papers/w15952

2. **How should business analysis adapt to the growing scale and opacity of private equity?**

    As private equity becomes a larger force in the economy, with increasingly complex ownership structures, leverage, and limited public disclosure, how can analysts accurately assess the incentives and financial risks affecting a privately owned company?

    I encountered this challenge while researching Blackboard / Anthology. Following the company through multiple ownership changes made it difficult to reconstruct the full economic picture from public information alone, particularly around private ownership, leverage, and the incentives of different financial sponsors. What analytical methods or sources does Eric recommend for understanding companies when the most consequential financial information is no longer publicly disclosed?

3. **How do we distinguish a company-specific failure from a systemic failure?**

    When analyzing a company, how should we account for broader forces in the financial and capital-market system—such as monetary policy, credit conditions, private-equity incentives, and the availability of leveraged financing—that may influence the company's ownership structure, leverage, and strategic decisions?

    I encountered this question while analyzing Blackboard / Anthology, where the company's financial trajectory was shaped not only by company-level decisions but also by the broader environment for leveraged finance and private-equity transactions. How can the framework distinguish between failures caused primarily by a company's own decisions and failures that are partly the result of broader financial-market structures and incentives?
