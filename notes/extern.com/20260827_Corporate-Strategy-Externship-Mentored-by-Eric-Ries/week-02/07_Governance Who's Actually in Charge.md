# Governance: Who's Actually in Charge?

*Estimated reading time: 25 minutes*

## Why This, Why Now?

Mission drive and resistance to financial gravity can change overnight with new ownership. Governance makes values durable. Before trusting a company's long-term trajectory, you need to understand the structural protections that defend its mission—and the vulnerabilities that expose it.

### From *Incorruptible*, Chapter 4

> Here's the truth: if a company cannot control its own destiny, it cannot be a trustworthy counterparty. Not to customers, not to employees, not even to the investors it supposedly serves.
>
> To put it as plainly as possible, if you don't get governance right, nothing else you do when building your products or your company will matter for the long-term. Any company can be bullied into submission by anyone who has enough money to mount a takeover or activist campaign. This isn't just rule by whoever is richest (which would be bad enough). It's also rule by whoever can borrow the most money.
>
> Sol Price, Robert Owen, the founders of Vectura—and two centuries of mission-driven builders, all betrayed by the same structural flaw. They proved their vision and their ethos worked, but they lacked the governance to protect it. Each betrayal didn't just destroy a company. It destroyed the trust of everyone who believed in that mission: employees, communities, even long-term investors.
>
> Either we fix governance, or every breakthrough becomes a breakdown and every transformative company becomes the next cautionary tale.

# The Four Pillars of Governance

Evaluate the company across four dimensions.

## Voting Structure

| Structure                                   | Protection Level | Characteristics                                                                |
| ------------------------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Dual-class (mission-aligned controller)** | High             | Founder or foundation controls the company despite minority economic ownership |
| **Dual-class (wealth-focused controller)**  | Medium           | Protection exists but may not serve the mission                                |
| **One share, one vote**                     | Low              | Standard vulnerability to share accumulation                                   |
| **Foundation/trust ownership**              | High             | Mission-locked by design                                                       |

### What to Look for in Proxy Statements

* "Class A" and "Class B" shares
* "Principal Stockholders" table
* "Voting Rights" section

## Board Composition

| Composition                             | Protection Level | Characteristics                              |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Mission-aligned majority**            | High             | Directors understand and defend the mission  |
| **Founder-controlled**                  | Medium           | Depends on founder alignment                 |
| **Long-tenured, independent**           | Medium           | Stability without a guaranteed mission focus |
| **Short-tenured, independent majority** | Low              | More susceptible to activist influence       |

### What to Look For

* Average director tenure
* Director backgrounds and affiliations
* Committee memberships
* Whether the CEO is also Board Chair

---

## Legal Structure

| Structure                            | Protection Level | What It Means                                                 |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Public Benefit Corporation (PBC)** | Medium+          | Legal obligation to consider stakeholders beyond shareholders |
| **B-Corp certified**                 | Low-Medium       | Certification of practices, not legal protection              |
| **Foundation ownership**             | High             | Mission-locked; examples include Novo Nordisk and Patagonia   |
| **Standard C-corp**                  | Low              | No structural mission protection                              |

### What to Look For

* State of incorporation
* Any mention of "benefit corporation" or "public benefit" in filings
* B-Corp certification in the B Corporation directory

---

## Takeover Defenses

| Defense                        | Protection Level | How It Works                                                   |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Poison pill**                | Medium           | Dilutes a hostile acquirer's stake                             |
| **Staggered board**            | Medium           | Only one-third of the board is up for election each year       |
| **Supermajority requirements** | Medium           | Requires a two-thirds or three-quarters vote for major changes |
| **Golden parachutes**          | Low              | Deters takeovers but is often criticized                       |
| **Combined defenses**          | High             | Multiple protections work together                             |

### What to Look For

* "Anti-takeover provisions" section in the proxy statement or 10-K
* "Stockholder Rights Plan" or poison pill language
* Board election terms: annual vs. staggered

# From the Book: The Vectura Case Study

### Chapter 4

> Vectura was founded in 1997 as a research spinout from the University of Bath. Professor of pharmaceutical technology John Staniforth and his team had dedicated their careers to improving inhaled medicines for respiratory diseases like asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). By 2021, Vectura was an independent public company trading on the London Stock Exchange, with more than a dozen approved therapies on the market.
>
> Philip Morris International (PMI) attempted to acquire Vectura for £1.1 billion. But it was actually only 165 pence per share. A private equity firm had also bid 155 pence per share. To prevent further bidders, PMI declared their intention to outbid any rival.
>
> Health charities begged them to say no. The British Thoracic Society called the potential deal "unethical." But Vectura's board? They unanimously accepted the bid from Philip Morris International, claiming that their fiduciary duty to shareholders left them no choice. Within three years, PMI had taken a $680 million write-down on its investment and sold the company's assets for a mere £150 million—a tenth of what they'd bid. It was an incredible act of value destruction.
>
> All for ten pence per share. This was my hypothetical made real: a company that helped people breathe, sold to a company that profited when they couldn't.

# The Four Responsibilities of Governance

### From *Incorruptible*, Chapter 4

Eric Ries argues that traditional governance teaches boards that their job is simple: maximize shareholder returns and don't break any laws. Real governance—"complete governance"—requires an exoskeleton with four load-bearing responsibilities.

**Compliance** means following laws, preventing self-dealing, reporting accurately, and holding management accountable. It is essential, but can become a "runaway system" that obsesses over procedures rather than protecting the organization.

**Purpose** means the legally binding commitment that defines what a company exists to maximize. It is not simply a mission statement, but a charter-level choice. There is no neutral position here—check the charter.

**Coherence** describes the degree to which an organization's systems, functions, and departments pursue a singular, unified direction. It concerns the alignment between stated values and cultural realities.

**Integrity** describes the degree to which an organization is protected from external forces that can force or tempt it to act contrary to its purpose. This is the domain of boards, partnerships, and investors—"both the highest authority and the guardian of the border with the outside world."

> "Under traditional governance, board meetings are dry affairs, often significantly divorced from the daily realities of employees, customers, and the communities they inhabit. But expand governance to include Coherence and Integrity, and the board's job becomes protecting and fulfilling the organization's true long-term purpose. It's frankly a lot more meaningful. Boards become guardians, not liquidators."

# Red Flags to Watch For

| Red Flag                                                      | Why It Matters                     |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **No controlling shareholder + one share, one vote**          | Easy takeover target               |
| **Board average tenure under three years**                    | Susceptible to activist pressure   |
| **Recent board turnover after activist pressure**             | Company has already capitulated    |
| **No poison pill or supermajority requirements**              | No structural barriers to change   |
| **CEO separate from founder, with no founder board presence** | Mission champion may have departed |
| **Large institutional holders with activist history**         | Pressure likely to intensify       |

---

# Green Flags to Look For

| Green Flag                               | Why It Matters                                |
| ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| **Founder retains voting control**       | Mission champion has structural power         |
| **Long-tenured, mission-aligned board**  | Continuity and shared values                  |
| **Public Benefit Corporation status**    | Legal cover for stakeholder focus             |
| **Foundation ownership stake**           | Mission-locked protection                     |
| **Survived activist campaign unchanged** | Proven resistance capability                  |
| **Staggered board + poison pill**        | Combined defenses make rapid change difficult |

# The Governance Spectrum

| Rating       | Definition                                                                           |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Fortress** | Multiple structural protections; cannot be taken over or changed against the mission |
| **Adequate** | Some protections in place; difficult but not impossible to change                    |
| **Weak**     | Few protections; vulnerable to activist pressure or acquisition                      |
| **Absent**   | No structural protection; company is whatever shareholders want                      |

# Apply This to Your Company

The interactive walkthrough will guide you through each step.

## Step 1: Identify Voting Structure

For public companies, find the proxy statement (DEF 14A) on SEC EDGAR.

**How to do this:** Ask Claude:

> "Does [Company] have dual-class shares? Who are the largest shareholders?"

For public companies, you can also search SEC EDGAR for the company's DEF 14A proxy statement, which lists major shareholders and voting structures. Dual-class shares are common in tech companies and give founders outsized control.

### Search For

* "Principal Stockholders" or "Security Ownership"
* "Voting Rights"
* Class A / Class B share descriptions

**Key question:** Is there a controlling shareholder? What percentage of votes do they control?

**Document:**

* Does this company have dual-class shares? (Yes/No/Unknown)
* If yes, who controls the voting shares?
* Top three shareholders and their approximate ownership percentages

---

## Step 2: Analyze Board Composition

In the proxy statement, find the "Directors" or "Board of Directors" section.

**How to do this:** Ask Perplexity:

> "Who are the board members of [Company] and how long has each served?"

Long-tenured boards with founder presence tend to be more mission-protective. Boards dominated by financial professionals or recently appointed directors may be more susceptible to short-term pressure or activist campaigns.

**Document:**

* Total number of directors
* Average tenure in years
* Number of independent directors
* Whether the CEO is also Board Chair (Yes/No)

**Key question:** Could this board be replaced through a proxy fight within 24 months?

---

## Step 3: Check Legal Structure

Determine whether the company has legal mission protection.

**How to do this:** Search the B Corporation directory to check B-Corp certification status. Ask Claude:

> "Is [Company] a Public Benefit Corporation?"

PBCs have legal obligations to consider stakeholders beyond shareholders. B-Corp certification and PBC status are different: a company can have either, both, or neither.

### Search For

* State of incorporation, usually Delaware
* "benefit corporation" or "public benefit" mentions
* B-Corp certification

**Document:**

* Is this a Public Benefit Corporation (PBC)? (Yes/No/Unknown)
* Is this company B-Corp certified? (Yes/No/Unknown)
* Any other legal structure notes, such as foundation ownership

**Key question:** Does the legal structure provide any mission protection?

---

## Step 4: Assess Takeover Defenses

In the proxy statement or 10-K, search for anti-takeover provisions.

**How to do this:** Ask Claude:

> "What anti-takeover provisions does [Company] have? Staggered board? Poison pill?"

A staggered board means only one-third of directors are up for election each year, making hostile takeovers require multiple years. These defenses can protect a mission while also entrenching poor leadership, making them a double-edged sword.

### Search Terms

* "Anti-takeover"
* "Stockholder Rights Plan" or "poison pill"
* "Supermajority"
* Board election terms: annual vs. staggered

**Document:**

* Does the company have a staggered board? (Yes/No/Unknown)
* Does the company have a poison pill? (Yes/No/Unknown)
* Other takeover defenses noted

**Key question:** What would it take for an activist to fundamentally change this company?

---

## Step 5: Calculate Takeover Cost

For public companies, estimate how expensive it would be to gain control.

**How to do this:** Search "[Company] market cap" to get the current valuation. Multiply by 10% to estimate an activist threshold position.

For context, activist funds such as Elliott Management typically have $50–100B under management. If a company's market cap is $500B, even a 10% stake ($50B) would be challenging for a single activist.

**Document:**

* Current market cap, if public
* Cost of a 10% stake
* Your 24-month vulnerability assessment

**Key question:** Is this company affordable to determined activists or acquirers?

---

## Step 6: Rate Governance

Based on your research, rate the company's governance protection.

**How to do this:** "Fortress" governance means an activist cannot force change because a founder controls a majority of votes. "Absent" means a well-funded activist could reshape the company within 24 months.

Consider the combination of ownership structure, board composition, legal structure, and market capitalization.

| Rating | Label        | Definition                                                             |
| -----: | ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
|  **5** | **Fortress** | Multiple structural protections; cannot be changed against the mission |
|  **4** | **Strong**   | Good protections; difficult but not impossible to change               |
|  **3** | **Partial**  | Some protections exist, but vulnerabilities remain                     |
|  **2** | **Weak**     | Few protections; vulnerable to activist pressure                       |
|  **1** | **Failed**   | No structural protection; company is whatever shareholders want        |

**Document:** Your rating and a one-sentence justification identifying the key governance factors.
