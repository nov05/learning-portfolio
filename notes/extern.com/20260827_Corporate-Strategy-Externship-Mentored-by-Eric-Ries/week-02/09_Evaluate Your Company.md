That matters for your mission test: **SAP gets paid when customers buy and continue using SAP products—not when SAP can demonstrate that it has literally “brought out the best” in those businesses.**

## 3. Could SAP profit while failing its mission?

### **Yes. This is the strongest reason to classify SAP as Mission-Hopeful.**

Imagine SAP completely stopped pursuing the idea of “bringing out the best in every business,” but continued to provide software that customers found useful enough to purchase.

SAP could still make money by:

* charging companies for cloud subscriptions;
* selling software support;
* selling licenses;
* providing consulting and implementation services;
* increasing cloud adoption among existing customers;
* selling additional products to its existing customer base.

There is **no direct revenue trigger requiring SAP to prove that each customer's business is objectively better as a result of using SAP**.

This is particularly clear from SAP's own description of its business model: revenue comes from **fees charged to customers** for cloud subscriptions, software licenses, support and services. ([SEC][4])

In 2025, SAP generated €21.0B in cloud revenue and €10.5B in software support revenue. ([SAP][3])

So the structural test is:

> **Can SAP make money without fulfilling “We bring out the best in every business”? Yes.**

That means the vision is **not structurally necessary for profitability**.

This differs from a company such as Costco, where particular mission-related practices can be deeply embedded in the mechanism that creates customer loyalty and revenue.

## 4. Has SAP chosen mission over profit—or the reverse?

There is **good evidence of SAP allowing its stated values to constrain revenue opportunities**, although not enough to establish that the entire business model is mission-driven.

### Evidence of choosing principles over revenue

SAP has a **Controversial Weapons Commitment Statement** that has been binding since 2023.

SAP explicitly states that it:

> will not conduct business with companies involved in the production or distribution of specified controversial weapons.

That means SAP has deliberately excluded certain potential customers from buying its software and services based on ethical criteria. SAP says this commitment goes **beyond legal requirements**. ([SAP][5])

That's a useful example for your assignment because it represents an actual **commercial constraint**:

**Potential revenue → ethical restriction → SAP refuses certain business.**

That is stronger evidence than simply saying “SAP cares about its mission.”

### Evidence pointing the other direction

SAP's 2024 restructuring provides evidence that **financial performance and strategic competitiveness remain major decision drivers**.

SAP announced a restructuring affecting approximately **8,000 positions**, explicitly describing the objectives as capturing organizational synergies, AI-driven efficiencies and preparing for scalable future revenue growth. The program was expected to involve approximately €2 billion in restructuring expenses. ([SAP][6])

SAP subsequently emphasized that the transformation was intended to shift resources toward strategic growth areas such as Business AI while improving efficiency and profitable growth. ([SAP][7])

I would **not** call the restructuring evidence that SAP violated its vision. But it does demonstrate that when SAP makes major strategic decisions, **growth, efficiency, profitability and competitiveness are explicit considerations**.

So the evidence is:

* **Mission/value over revenue:** refusing certain controversial-weapons-related customers.
* **Business/financial priorities:** restructuring to improve efficiency and redirect resources toward growth areas.

That combination supports **Mission-Hopeful**, rather than Mission-Driven.

**Ownership:** SAP is a publicly traded company, not PE-owned or VC-backed. Its major shareholders include large institutional investors such as BlackRock.

**Primary pressure source: Public-market growth and profitability expectations.**

SAP's shareholders expect continued growth, but its core ERP business is already highly mature after decades of market dominance. This makes sustained growth increasingly difficult and pushes SAP toward cloud, AI, and adjacent businesses.

Three pressures are especially important:

* **Mature ERP market:** SAP has a huge installed base, but this also limits organic growth opportunities.
* **AI disruption:** Generative AI lowers the cost of developing software, making it easier for smaller companies to build ERP or ERP-like products that could compete with SAP.
* **Long-term commoditization:** Core ERP may remain essential for large companies for decades, similar to banking's legacy systems, but the profits from providing these systems could decline as software development and infrastructure become cheaper and more commoditized.

**Why this is the primary pressure:** Unlike PE-owned companies, SAP does not face a sponsor demanding debt repayment or an exit. Unlike VC-backed startups, it is not primarily driven by rapid-growth funding milestones. Its main pressure comes from public-market investors expecting **continued growth, profitability, and shareholder value**.

**Key tension:** SAP's strong ERP installed base provides stability and high switching costs, but its maturity makes growth harder while AI and commoditization could gradually weaken its historical competitive advantage.
