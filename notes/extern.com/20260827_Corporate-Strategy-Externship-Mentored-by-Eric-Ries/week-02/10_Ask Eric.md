# Ask Eric

Your Task

Reflect on your Incorruptible Scorecard experience and formulate a specific, thoughtful question for Eric Ries tied to your company analysis.

Estimated time: 15 minutes

Reflection Prompts

Before writing your question, consider:

* What surprised you during your analysis that you didn't expect?
* Where did you struggle to find information or make a judgment call?
* What patterns did you notice that you'd like Eric's perspective on?
* What would you do differently if you were actually evaluating this company for a job offer?

What Makes a Good Question

Strong questions are:

* Specific — Tied to your actual analysis experience, not generic
* Grounded in evidence — Reference what you found or couldn't find
* Actionable — Seeking insight you can apply to future evaluations

Examples of strong questions:

> "When analyzing [Company], I found conflicting signals—strong mission alignment but concerning investor pressure. How do you weigh these tradeoffs when the founder seems committed but external pressures are mounting?"

> "I struggled to assess [Company]'s governance because they're pre-IPO with limited public information. What signals should early-stage job seekers look for when traditional red flags aren't visible yet?"

Examples of weak questions:

* "What do you think about [Company]?" — Too vague
* "Is dual-class stock bad?" — Not tied to your specific analysis
* "How do I know if a company is good?" — Too broad

Question Category: Week 2 - Incorruptible Scorecard   

```text
When applying the Incorruptible Scorecard to a large, mature company like SAP or an S&P 500 company, how should we distinguish genuine mission protection from institutional inertia? These companies may be difficult to disrupt or replace simply because of their scale, installed customer base, and governance structure. What signals would tell us that a company is actually resisting short-term pressure in service of its mission, rather than simply being too large and entrenched to change?
```

```text
When evaluating a strategically important company like Intel, where government ownership and national-security considerations increasingly shape its strategy, should we still evaluate it primarily as a free-market company using the Incorruptible Scorecard, or do we need a different framework once the state becomes a meaningful stakeholder?
```

Note: Intel is an interesting case because its governance and strategic pressures extend beyond ordinary shareholder interests. Bob Swan, who had a finance background, served as CEO from 2019 to 2021 before Pat Gelsinger, an engineering and technology leader, became CEO; after Gelsinger’s departure, Intel appointed Lip-Bu Tan, a semiconductor and technology executive, as CEO in March 2025. In August 2025, the U.S. government agreed to invest $8.9 billion for a 9.9% stake in Intel, explicitly linking the investment to U.S. technology, manufacturing, and national-security priorities, while remaining a passive shareholder without board representation. This raises a broader question about whether companies that have become strategically important to the state should still be evaluated primarily through a conventional free-market framework.
